# Guidance for AI Adoption (AI6)

**Publisher:** National AI Centre (NAIC), Department of Industry, Science and Resources (DISR)
**Published:** October 2025
**Last Updated:** October 2025
**Status:** Current — replaces the Voluntary AI Safety Standard (VAISS)
**Applies to:** All Australian organisations (voluntary; primary audience is industry, business, and the broader economy)
**Source:** [industry.gov.au](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/guidance-for-ai-adoption)

---

## What This Is

The Guidance for AI Adoption (informally referred to as "AI6") is the Australian Government's primary voluntary guidance framework for responsible AI adoption across industry. Published in October 2025 by the National AI Centre under DISR, it formally replaces the Voluntary AI Safety Standard (VAISS), which had been in place since 2024 with its 10 guardrails.

AI6 is not regulation. It is voluntary guidance, but it is the document that government, investors, and enterprise procurement teams will reference when asking whether an organisation has a responsible AI governance approach. It explicitly aligns with international standards including ISO/IEC 42001 and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework.

---

## Structure

The guidance is split into two parts designed for different organisational maturity levels:

**Part 1 — Foundations:** Aimed at small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and organisations new to AI governance. Covers the basic concepts, obligations, and starting points for responsible AI adoption.

**Part 2 — Implementation Practices:** Aimed at larger or more mature organisations. Provides more detailed, operationally-oriented guidance on implementing each of the six practices.

A supporting suite of resources accompanies the guidance, including an AI screening tool, policy and register templates, and reference materials on risk mitigation.

---

## The Six Responsible AI Practices

AI6 consolidates the VAISS's 10 guardrails into six responsible AI practices. These are:

**1. Governance and Accountability**
Establish clear roles, responsibilities, and decision-making authority for AI systems. This includes board-level or senior management accountability, defined processes for approving AI use cases, and a governance structure that covers the full AI lifecycle.

**2. Impact Assessment**
Assess the potential impacts of AI systems before and during deployment. This covers harms to individuals and groups, unintended consequences, fairness considerations, and the rights of those affected by AI decisions. Impact assessments should be proportionate to the risk level of the AI system.

**3. Risk Management**
Identify, assess, and treat risks associated with AI systems throughout their lifecycle. This includes technical risks (model performance, drift, adversarial inputs), operational risks (data quality, supply chain), and broader societal risks (bias, discrimination, safety).

**4. Transparency**
Be transparent with users, affected parties, and the public about how AI is being used, what decisions it informs or makes, and what its limitations are. Transparency includes both internal transparency (governance documentation) and external transparency (user-facing communication).

**5. Testing and Monitoring**
Test AI systems before deployment and monitor them continuously in operation. This covers accuracy, robustness, fairness testing, and ongoing performance monitoring to detect degradation, drift, or unexpected behaviour.

**6. Human Oversight**
Ensure humans retain meaningful oversight and control of AI systems. The appropriate level of oversight is proportionate to risk — high-risk AI decisions affecting individuals require stronger human-in-the-loop mechanisms than low-risk internal tools.

## How It Maps to ISO 42001

The alignment between AI6 and ISO 42001 is strong and deliberate — the guidance was designed with international standards compatibility in mind.

| AI6 Practice | ISO 42001 Mapping |
|-------------|-------------------|
| Governance and Accountability | Clause 5 (Leadership), Annex A Category 5 (Policies), Annex A 6.1–6.2 (Roles) |
| Impact Assessment | Clause 8.2 (AI System Impact Assessment) |
| Risk Management | Clause 6.1–6.3 (Planning / Risk), Annex A 8.1–8.6 (Lifecycle) |
| Transparency | Annex A 5.1, 10.1 (Documentation), Clause 7.4 (Communication) |
| Testing and Monitoring | Annex A 8.3 (Verification), Clause 9.1 (Monitoring) |
| Human Oversight | Annex A 9.1–9.3 (Human Oversight) |

**Practical implication:** An organisation implementing ISO 42001 will satisfy the intent of AI6's six practices through their AIMS controls. AI6 can be used as a communication tool — it is the language Australian government and industry stakeholders use, while ISO 42001 is the auditable governance structure underneath it.

---

## What It Doesn't Do

- AI6 is not regulation and creates no legal obligations
- It does not provide certification — organisations cannot be "AI6 certified"
- It does not define specific technical standards for AI model development
- It does not address sector-specific regulatory requirements (APRA, TGA, ASIC etc.) — those are separate

---

## Practitioner Notes

If you are talking to an Australian government agency, board, or enterprise customer about AI governance, AI6 is the framework they will know. Mapping your ISO 42001 AIMS to AI6's six practices is a worthwhile exercise — it produces a communication layer that translates your formal governance into language your Australian audience will immediately recognise.

The AI screening tool published alongside AI6 is worth reviewing as a starting point for identifying which AI use cases require deeper governance attention.
